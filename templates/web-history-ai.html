Introduction

The World Wide Web is an information system that runs on top of the Internet, letting documents be published, addressed and linked. The Internet is the network infrastructure; the Web is one application of it, and email and file transfer predate it.

Tim Berners-Lee invented the Web at CERN between 1989 and 1991, on three inventions of his own: HTML, HTTP and the URL. What followed was a layered build-out of scripting, style sheets, standards work, accessibility guidelines and default encryption. Every date below was verified against the sources listed with it.

Milestone timeline

  1. The proposal, the first server and browser — 1989–1990

    Berners-Lee proposed the system at CERN in March 1989, and from October 1990 wrote the first web server and the first browser-editor on a NeXT machine.

    Why it mattered: by Christmas 1990 he had defined HTML, HTTP and URLs, which remain the Web's foundation.

    Source: CERN — A short history of the Web, home.cern

  2. The Web goes public — 1991

    A portable Line-Mode browser reached CERN users in March 1991, and on 6 August 1991 the software was announced on Internet newsgroups.

    Why it mattered: info.cern.ch became the first website, and the Web moved beyond one laboratory.

    Source: CERN — A short history of the Web, home.cern

  3. W3C founded — 1994

    Berners-Lee established the World Wide Web Consortium at MIT in October 1994, and over 50 companies joined at once.

    Why it mattered: a neutral standards body kept the Web interoperable rather than fragmented by proprietary features.

    Source: W3C — About: history, w3.org

  4. JavaScript — 1995

    Brendan Eich wrote the language at Netscape; it shipped as LiveScript in November and was renamed JavaScript that December.

    Why it mattered: pages could now respond to the user without contacting the server, which made web applications possible.

    Source: Computer Hope — Computer programming history, computerhope.com

  5. Cascading Style Sheets — 1996

    CSS Level 1 became a W3C Recommendation in December 1996, from Håkon Wium Lie's 1994 proposal merged with Bert Bos's work.

    Why it mattered: separating presentation from content gave authors, not browser vendors, control over layout.

    Source: Håkon Wium Lie — PhD thesis on Cascading Style Sheets, wiumlie.no

  6. HTML 4.01 and WCAG 1.0 — 1999

    HTML 4.01 became a Recommendation in December 1999; WCAG 1.0 was published on 5 May 1999.

    Why it mattered: HTML 4.01 was the dominant markup standard for a decade, and WCAG established accessibility as a requirement of the Web.

    Source: WebAIM — Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, webaim.org

  7. XHTML, WHATWG and WCAG 2.0 — 2000–2008

    XHTML 1.0 recast HTML in strict XML syntax in 2000; after a 2004 W3C workshop voted against evolving HTML, Apple, Mozilla and Opera formed WHATWG and began HTML5, and WCAG 2.0 followed in December 2008.

    Why it mattered: HTML5 restarted markup development on a backward-compatible footing, while WCAG 2.0 introduced the POUR principles.

    Source: WHATWG — HTML Standard, history section, html.spec.whatwg.org

  8. Responsive web design — 2010

    Ethan Marcotte coined the term on 6 April 2010 and published the article on 25 May, uniting fluid grids, flexible images and media queries.

    Why it mattered: one site could serve every screen size instead of a separate mobile version.

    Source: Ethan Marcotte — Responsive web design turns ten, ethanmarcotte.com

  9. HTML5 becomes a Recommendation — 2014

    Published on 28 October 2014 with semantic elements such as article, section, header and nav, plus APIs for video and offline use.

    Why it mattered: it aligned the standard with what browsers and authors actually did, and removed the need for plug-ins.

    Source: W3C — HTML5 Differences from HTML4, w3.org

  10. HTTPS becomes the default — 2015–2018

    Let's Encrypt launched free automated certificates in 2015, and Chrome marked every plain HTTP site "Not secure" in July 2018.

    Why it mattered: encryption moved from optional to expected, protecting ordinary browsing from interception.

    Source: Google Transparency Report — HTTPS encryption on the web, transparencyreport.google.com

  11. WCAG 2.1 — 2018

    Published on 5 June 2018, adding 17 success criteria without replacing WCAG 2.0.

    Why it mattered: it extended accessibility to touchscreens, small screens, low vision and cognitive disability.

    Source: W3C — WCAG 2 Overview, w3.org

  12. HTTP/3 becomes a standard — 2022

    The IETF published HTTP/3 as RFC 9114 in June 2022, moving the Web's transport from TCP to QUIC over UDP.

    Why it mattered: it removed the head-of-line blocking that slowed HTTP/2 on lossy networks and let connections survive a switch between Wi-Fi and mobile data.

    Source: IETF — RFC 9114: HTTP/3, rfc-editor.org

  13. WCAG 2.2 — 2023

    Published on 5 October 2023, adding nine success criteria covering focus appearance, dragging alternatives, minimum target size, consistent help and accessible authentication.

    Why it mattered: it addressed touch and cognitive barriers that WCAG 2.1 left open, and it is now the benchmark referenced in accessibility law and procurement.

    Source: W3C — What's New in WCAG 2.2, w3.org
