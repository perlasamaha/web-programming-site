Introduction

The World Wide Web is an information system that runs on top of the Internet, letting documents be published, addressed and linked. The Internet is the network infrastructure; the Web is one application of it, and email and file transfer predate it.

Tim Berners-Lee invented the Web at CERN between 1989 and 1991, on three inventions of his own: HTML, HTTP and the URL. What followed was a layered build-out of scripting, style sheets, standards work, accessibility guidelines and default encryption. Every date below was verified against the source cited beneath it.

Milestone timeline

  1. 1989-1990 — The proposal, the first server and browser

    Berners-Lee proposed the system at CERN in March 1989, and from October 1990 wrote the first web server and the first browser-editor on a NeXT machine. Why it mattered: by Christmas 1990 he had defined HTML, HTTP and URLs, which remain the Web's foundation.

    Source: CERN — A short history of the Web: https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

  2. 1991 — The Web goes public

    A portable Line-Mode browser reached CERN users in March 1991, and on 6 August 1991 the software was announced on Internet newsgroups. Why it mattered: info.cern.ch became the first website, and the Web moved beyond one laboratory.

    Source: CERN — A short history of the Web: https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

  3. 1994 — W3C founded

    Berners-Lee established the World Wide Web Consortium at MIT in October 1994, and over 50 companies joined at once. Why it mattered: a neutral standards body kept the Web interoperable rather than fragmented by proprietary features.

    Source: W3C — About: history: https://w3.org/about/history

  4. 1995 — JavaScript

    Brendan Eich wrote the language at Netscape; it shipped as LiveScript in November and was renamed JavaScript that December. Why it mattered: pages could now respond to the user without contacting the server, which made web applications possible.

    Source: Computer Hope — Computer programming history: https://www.computerhope.com/history/programming.htm

  5. 1996 — Cascading Style Sheets

    CSS Level 1 became a W3C Recommendation in December 1996, from Håkon Wium Lie's 1994 proposal merged with Bert Bos's work. Why it mattered: separating presentation from content gave authors, not browser vendors, control over layout.

    Source: Håkon Wium Lie — PhD thesis on Cascading Style Sheets: https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/css.pdf

  6. 1999 — HTML 4.01 and WCAG 1.0

    HTML 4.01 became a Recommendation in December 1999; WCAG 1.0 was published on 5 May 1999. Why it mattered: HTML 4.01 was the dominant markup standard for a decade, and WCAG established accessibility as a requirement of the Web.

    Source: WebAIM — Web Content Accessibility Guidelines: https://webaim.org/standards/wcag/

  7. 2000-2008 — XHTML, WHATWG and WCAG 2.0

    XHTML 1.0 recast HTML in strict XML syntax in 2000; after a 2004 W3C workshop voted against evolving HTML, Apple, Mozilla and Opera formed WHATWG and began HTML5, and WCAG 2.0 followed in December 2008. Why it mattered: HTML5 restarted markup development on a backward-compatible footing, while WCAG 2.0 introduced the POUR principles.

    Source: WHATWG — HTML Standard, history section: https://html.spec.whatwg.org/

  8. 2010 — Responsive web design

    Ethan Marcotte coined the term on 6 April 2010 and published the article on 25 May, uniting fluid grids, flexible images and media queries. Why it mattered: one site could serve every screen size instead of a separate mobile version.

    Source: Ethan Marcotte — Responsive web design turns ten: https://ethanmarcotte.com/wrote/responsive-design-at-10/

  9. 2014 — HTML5 becomes a Recommendation

    Published on 28 October 2014 with semantic elements such as article, section, header and nav, plus APIs for video and offline use. Why it mattered: it aligned the standard with what browsers and authors actually did, and removed the need for plug-ins.

    Source: W3C — HTML5 Differences from HTML4: https://www.w3.org/TR/html5-diff/

  10. 2015-2018 — HTTPS becomes the default

    Let's Encrypt launched free automated certificates in 2015, and Chrome marked every plain HTTP site "Not secure" in July 2018. Why it mattered: encryption moved from optional to expected, protecting ordinary browsing from interception.

    Source: Google Transparency Report — HTTPS encryption on the web: https://transparencyreport.google.com/https/certificates

  11. 2018 — WCAG 2.1

    Published on 5 June 2018, adding 17 success criteria without replacing WCAG 2.0. Why it mattered: it extended accessibility to touchscreens, small screens, low vision and cognitive disability.

    Source: W3C — WCAG 2 Overview: https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/
